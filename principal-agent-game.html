Delegation and the principal-agent game

Voters delegate to legislators, legislators to a government, ministers to civil servants. Each time, someone hands power to an agent who may not do what they want. The guided walkthrough sets out the chapter’s principal-agent game, works through its three scenarios from Figure 11.7, and then covers the information problems behind delegation and the ways principals deal with them. Explore lets you move the status quo and both ideal points yourself and see what the agent can get away with. Practice has two parts: solving the game, with new scenarios generated without end, and problems and fixes, eleven delegation scenarios in which you identify what the principal is worried about and the mechanism they used.

The principal accepts any proposal that leaves them no worse off than the status quo, including one they are exactly indifferent to. The region of acceptability can run off the end of the 0–10 scale; the agent’s proposal never does, since the agent never wants a policy beyond its own ideal point.

Practice

New scenarios are generated without end, each with its own status quo and ideal points. Every scenario is one of four kinds, listed here. A kind is checked off once you solve a scenario of that kind with every answer right the first time, and new scenarios favor the kinds you haven’t checked off yet.

Kinds of scenario

    1. What is the principal’s region of acceptability?

    2. What policy does the agent propose?

    3. What is the final policy outcome?

    4. How large is the principal’s agency loss?

    5. Was delegation successful?

    6. How much power did the agent have as agenda setter?

    Eleven delegation scenarios. For each one, decide what the principal is worried about (adverse selection or moral hazard) and which kind of mechanism they used to deal with it: screening or selection before the agent is chosen, or a police patrol or fire alarm afterward. Each of the four mechanisms appears more than once.
