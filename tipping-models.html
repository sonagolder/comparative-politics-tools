Tipping models

Popular revolutions tend to surprise everyone, including the people who take part in them. Eastern Europe in 1989 and Tunisia in 2010–11 both looked stable until shortly before their governments fell. Timur Kuran's (1991) tipping model helps explain why. Each of ten people has a revolutionary threshold: how many others must already be protesting before they will join. Everyone can see who is protesting, but nobody can see how close anyone else is to joining.

    Each person's revolutionary threshold

    A revolutionary threshold is how many other people must already be protesting before a person will join in. Bigger protests make it harder for the state to identify and punish any one protester, so joining gets less costly as a protest grows. Thresholds vary from person to person. Dissidents have a threshold of 0 and protest whatever anyone else does. Frightened people need many others out first. People who support the regime are unlikely to join under almost any circumstances. In a ten-person society a threshold of 10 means never, since at most nine others can be protesting.

    0510

    The rule. Everyone whose threshold is at or below the number already protesting joins. That raises the number, which may bring in more people, and so on until nobody new is willing to join.

      Compare A′ with C. The average threshold in C is 2.6, and in A′ it is 4.6. Most people in C are much closer to joining a protest, yet C stays quiet while A′ has a nine-person protest. What matters is how thresholds are distributed across the society, not how low they are on average.

      Why revolutions come as a surprise

      Opposing a dictatorship in public is dangerous, so people who oppose the regime often support it in public instead. This is preference falsification. One result is that nobody knows the true level of opposition, because everyone appears to support the regime.

      Preference falsification also means that a society's distribution of revolutionary thresholds is never known to the people in it. There are two layers to this. Public support lumps together the regime's genuine supporters and its opponents who are falsifying their preferences, and watching someone stay home cannot tell you which they are. And even among those who are falsifying, nobody can see how close each one is to joining a protest. Knowing who is falsifying would not be enough: societies A and B could have exactly the same people falsifying, and differ only in whether person 3 needs two others protesting or three. Society C, where the regime is deeply unpopular, shows that widespread preference falsification does not by itself produce a cascade. A society can come to the brink of revolution without anyone knowing it. People may be ready to join a full-scale revolt if just one more person goes out to protest. If that person stays home, there is no revolution and the dictatorship looks entirely stable. Even people who know preference falsification is going on cannot tell whether they live in a society like A or one like B. That is why it is often impossible to tell very stable dictatorships from very fragile ones.

      So revolutions will nearly always come as a surprise, which Kuran (1989, 1991) calls the "predictability of unpredictability." This does not make revolutions irrational or beyond understanding. Failing to predict them is, in Kuran's words, "entirely consistent with calculated, purposeful human action" (1991, 45).

      The tool's "is this society on the brink" panel and its threshold numbers show what nobody inside a society can see. Try using them in that spirit: look only at how many people are protesting, decide whether you would have expected a revolution, and then look at the thresholds.

      Why revolutions look inevitable afterward

      Once a revolutionary cascade grows into an overwhelming majority, staying a public supporter of the government becomes unwise. Some of the regime's supporters join the protests even though they would have preferred them to fail. The chapter calls this a revolutionary bandwagon, to distinguish it from a revolutionary cascade.

      After the regime falls, preference falsification runs the other way. People who supported the old regime now have reason to say they had always opposed it. Interviews conducted after 1989 across Eastern Europe found that nearly everyone said they had opposed communist rule. That makes the old dictatorship look much weaker and more unloved than it was, and the revolution look inevitable. As Kuran puts it, preference falsification makes it "even less comprehensible why the revolution was unforeseen" (1991, 23).

      Structural factors and the spark

      Some explanations treat revolutions as caused by structural factors such as relative deprivation, grievances, or oppression (Gurr 1970). The tipping model suggests these factors can lower thresholds without producing a revolution, as in society C. Structural factors can make revolutions more likely, but they do not make them inevitable. What they can do is create conditions in which an idiosyncratic event becomes the start of a revolution. In Tunisia in December 2010, Mohamed Bouazizi's self-immolation after mistreatment by local officials began the Arab Spring. A similar act where thresholds had not been lowered might have had few consequences beyond the people directly affected.

      In Eastern Europe, the chapter points to several changes during the 1980s that lowered thresholds. Gorbachev's reformist policies, perestroika and glasnost, reduced the perceived risk of challenging the status quo. Many Eastern European economies performed increasingly poorly from the mid-1980s. In 1989 Gorbachev said that the Soviet Union would not intervene militarily to keep communist rule in place in Eastern Europe. Once reforms succeeded in one country, they had demonstration effects in the others, lowering thresholds further, so the cascade ran between countries as well as within them. A banner at a 1989 rally in Prague put it this way: "Poland—10 years, Hungary—10 months, East Germany—10 weeks, Czechoslovakia—10 days" (Garton Ash 1999).
