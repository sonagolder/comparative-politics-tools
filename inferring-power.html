Inferring power from behavior

In the exit, voice, and loyalty game, if you know a citizen’s exit payoff and a government’s dependence, solving the model tells you what each will do. Here you start with the outcome. You are handed a record of what happened and asked what may legitimately be concluded from it about who held power. This is more challenging, because different balances of power can produce exactly the same visible behavior.

Citizen and government are the model’s names for the two roles — the actor something is taken from, and the actor that takes it. The cases put all kinds of actors in those positions.

One assumption runs underneath all of it: the citizen would rather get the benefit back, even after paying the cost of speaking up, than leave. Without it she would simply leave every time, whatever the government might do, and there would be nothing to work out.

A benefit has been taken. What follows?

The game as the chapter presents it. In every case the government has already made a negative change to the citizen's environment, and the question is what her response reveals.

Is a benefit taken at all?

The extended game, which adds a first move: the government decides whether to take the benefit in the first place. It does not add a fifth prediction — it replaces one, so read these cases against the extension rather than the game above.

When the players don't know

So far the game has assumed each side already knows everything about the other: the government knows whether the citizen can really leave, and she knows whether it depends on her. Each of these records leaves something that assumption cannot account for. Allowing the two sides to be uncertain about each other is what explains them.
