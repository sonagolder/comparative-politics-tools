The d'Hondt divisor method

A divisor system turns votes into seats without a quota. Each party's votes are divided by 1, 2, 3 and so on, and the seats go to the largest of the results, one at a time, until they run out. Table 12.8 of the chapter works the d'Hondt method through on the same district as the Hare quota example. Step through it one row at a time, then pick the seats yourself for districts you have not seen.

            How it is checked

            The quotients are worked out for you. Each one is a button: press it to give that quotient a seat, and press it again to take the seat away. Inside the table, the arrow keys move between quotients.

            Step 1. Pick the quotients that win seats, then check. If something is wrong, the quotients you picked are marked and the tool says what kind of mistake it looks like. Change your picks and check again as often as you like, or ask to be shown the seats and go on.

            Step 2. Once the seats are right, fill in each party's total seats and check. This check is once only; afterwards the order the seats were won in and the ranking of quotients appear.

            The table holds enough rows to show where the seats run out, and sometimes a row or two more, so the number of rows does not give the answer away. The method itself is on the other tab, worked through step by step.

            Three things worth noticing

            Every district shows at least one of these. Each is checked off once you get both steps right at the first check, without being shown anything, in a district that has it. Districts lean toward whichever you have not got yet.

              All three covered. You have now seen each thing this method does that catches people out. The districts do not run out, so keep going for as long as it is useful, but from here they are practice on cases you have already met.

              How far down the table to go

              You never need to divide a party's votes by every number up to the seat count. Once a party's next quotient is smaller than the last quotient that wins a seat, nothing further down its column can win.

              Why d'Hondt tends to help larger parties

              The smallest quotient that wins a seat works like a price. Votes left over after the last full price buy nothing, however close they come, and leftovers are a bigger share of a small party's votes than of a large party's.

              How the quotients are shown

              Quotients are shown rounded to the nearest whole vote, so 47,000 ÷ 3 appears as 15,667 (Table 12.8 prints 15,666). Rounding never changes which of two quotients is larger, and the ranking always uses the exact values.

              Where these numbers come from

              The method and the ten-seat example are from Chapter 12 of Principles of Comparative Politics, 4th edition (Table 12.8). The other ready-made districts and every practice district were made up for this tool. They are not the results of any real election, and none of them is one of the chapter's end-of-chapter problems.

              Practice districts are generated, not chosen. Each one has a clear gap between the last quotient that wins a seat and the next one, so no district turns on a near tie.
